## Mastery Objective

At senior architecture level, influence extends beyond project delivery.

The architect may need to:

- discover an opportunity,
- shape a client problem,
- build a solution narrative,
- defend architecture,
- quantify value,
- manage commercial constraints,
- lead a pursuit,
- make decisions under uncertainty,
- and create confidence without overpromising.

Presales is not “selling technology.”

Leadership is not “having authority.”

Decision-making is not “being certain.”

The architecture leadership model is:

**Discover → Qualify → Shape → Prove → Decide → Commit → Lead.**

# 20 Scenario-Based Interview Questions

## 1. Client says, “Why should we transform Finance?”

**Question:** How would you lead the first executive conversation?

**S — Situation:** A prospective client recognized Finance pain but had not defined a transformation case.

**T — Task:** I needed to help leadership understand whether transformation was necessary and what outcomes mattered.

**A — Action:** I explored close cycle, manual effort, control risk, data trust, reporting latency, integration complexity, operating cost, growth constraints, and strategic objectives. I framed potential transformation outcomes before discussing products.

**R — Result:** The conversation moved from technology interest toward a business-value discussion.

**SME Probe:** What should come before a product presentation?

**Reflection:** Good presales begins with the client's problem, not the vendor's catalog.

---

## 2. Client asks for an unrealistic proposal

**Question:** A client wants global Finance transformation, full automation, and go-live in four months. What do you do?

**S:** The requested scope and timeline appeared inconsistent with the transformation complexity.

**T:** I needed to remain commercially useful without overpromising.

**A:** I decomposed scope, dependencies, data migration, integration, regulatory needs, organizational readiness, and critical path. I created phased options with explicit assumptions, risks, and minimum viable outcomes.

**R:** Leadership could choose a realistic path with transparent trade-offs.

**SME Probe:** Why is saying “yes” too early dangerous in presales?

**Reflection:** A short-term sales win can become a long-term delivery failure.

---

## 3. Build a solution for a global Finance RFP

**Question:** How would you approach an RFP response?

**S:** A multinational issued an RFP for Finance transformation.

**T:** I needed to create a differentiated, credible response.

**A:** I analyzed business requirements, evaluation criteria, architecture constraints, scope, dependencies, regulatory complexity, integration landscape, delivery model, and value expectations. I structured the response around outcomes, capabilities, architecture, delivery approach, governance, risks, and evidence.

**R:** The response could be evaluated as a coherent transformation proposition rather than a list of product features.

**SME Probe:** What makes an RFP response credible?

**Reflection:** Credibility comes from traceability between requirement, solution, delivery, and outcome.

---

## 4. Client asks for the “cheapest” solution

**Question:** How do you respond when price is the primary selection criterion?

**S:** A client prioritized lowest initial cost.

**T:** I needed to make total business and lifecycle economics visible.

**A:** I compared initial investment with operating cost, integration complexity, support, technical debt, change effort, scalability, risk, migration cost, and exit implications.

**R:** The client could evaluate price in the context of total value and lifecycle consequences.

**SME Probe:** Is the cheapest solution always the lowest-cost solution?

**Reflection:** Acquisition cost and lifecycle economics are different questions.

---

## 5. Client asks for a product-first demo

**Question:** A prospect wants a two-hour SAP demo before sharing business context.

**S:** The prospect wanted to evaluate product functionality immediately.

**T:** I needed to make the demonstration relevant rather than generic.

**A:** I requested representative business scenarios, user personas, pain points, process variations, and decision criteria. I designed the demo around those scenarios and explicitly connected features to outcomes.

**R:** The demonstration became evidence for business decisions rather than a feature tour.

**SME Probe:** What makes a good presales demo?

**Reflection:** Demonstrate the client's future, not the product's menu.

---

## 6. Client challenges your architecture in a bid defense

**Question:** How would you defend a target architecture when challenged?

**S:** A client challenged the proposed Finance architecture during a competitive bid.

**T:** I needed to demonstrate why the design addressed the stated requirements.

**A:** I connected each major decision to requirements, architecture principles, constraints, trade-offs, risks, and expected outcomes. Where alternatives were valid, I acknowledged them and explained the selection criteria.

**R:** The architecture could withstand scrutiny without relying on unsupported claims.

**SME Probe:** What should you do if another architecture is genuinely better for one requirement?

**Reflection:** Strong leadership is not threatened by evidence that changes the decision.

---

## 7. Commercial team promises a feature

**Question:** Sales promises a capability that architecture has not validated.

**S:** A commercial commitment had been made before technical feasibility was confirmed.

**T:** I needed to protect client trust and delivery integrity.

**A:** I validated the requirement, assessed standard capability, configuration, extension, integration, roadmap, and constraints. I documented what was supported, what required additional work, and what assumptions applied.

**R:** The commitment could be corrected or bounded before becoming a delivery problem.

**SME Probe:** How should architects work with sales teams?

**Reflection:** Architecture must enable commercial momentum without sacrificing truth.

---

## 8. Pursuit has limited time

**Question:** You have three days to shape a complex Finance proposal. What do you prioritize?

**S:** A strategic opportunity had a compressed proposal timeline.

**T:** I needed to focus effort on decision-critical content.

**A:** I prioritized client outcomes, critical requirements, architecture principles, differentiators, delivery risks, dependencies, assumptions, solution shape, value proposition, and proof points. I avoided low-value documentation.

**R:** The team produced a coherent proposal under time pressure.

**SME Probe:** What should not be sacrificed under a tight timeline?

**Reflection:** Speed should reduce unnecessary work, not decision quality.

---

## 9. Client asks, “What is your unique value?”

**Question:** How would you articulate architecture differentiation?

**S:** A client asked why the consulting approach was different from competitors.

**T:** I needed to explain value without generic claims.

**A:** I connected differentiation to measurable capabilities such as industry context, reusable architecture patterns, accelerated discovery, migration experience, integration discipline, quality assurance, AI governance, and outcome measurement.

**R:** Differentiation became evidence-based rather than marketing language.

**SME Probe:** What makes a differentiator credible?

**Reflection:** A differentiator must change the client's probability of achieving a better outcome.

---

## 10. Executive decision has incomplete data

**Question:** Leadership wants a decision today but important information is missing.

**S:** A major Finance architecture decision was time-sensitive.

**T:** I needed to enable a decision without pretending uncertainty did not exist.

**A:** I separated known facts, assumptions, unknowns, reversible decisions, irreversible decisions, and decision thresholds. I recommended proceeding only where risk was bounded and defined the evidence required for later validation.

**R:** Leadership could act while explicitly managing uncertainty.

**SME Probe:** What makes a decision reversible?

**Reflection:** Good leaders distinguish uncertainty that can be learned from uncertainty that must be resolved first.

---

## 11. Program is failing and leadership asks you to take over

**Question:** How would you approach a troubled Finance transformation?

**S:** The program had missed milestones and stakeholders had lost confidence.

**T:** I needed to stabilize the program before redesigning it.

**A:** I assessed scope, outcomes, governance, architecture, dependencies, delivery health, data, integration, risks, decision backlog, stakeholder confidence, and adoption. I established immediate stabilization actions and a recovery roadmap.

**R:** Leadership gained visibility into root causes and a structured path toward recovery.

**SME Probe:** Why should a recovery start with diagnosis?

**Reflection:** Changing the plan without understanding failure modes can repeat the same mistakes.

---

## 12. Executive team disagrees on investment

**Question:** CFO wants automation; COO wants process simplification; CIO wants platform modernization.

**S:** Multiple executives wanted different transformation investments.

**T:** I needed to create an enterprise decision model.

**A:** I mapped each initiative to shared outcomes, dependencies, risk reduction, capability impact, investment, and sequencing. I showed where one initiative enabled another.

**R:** Leadership could see the portfolio as an interconnected transformation rather than competing departmental projects.

**SME Probe:** Why is portfolio architecture important?

**Reflection:** Enterprise value often exists between initiatives, not inside one initiative.

## 13. Client asks for a fixed-price transformation

**Question:** What architectural considerations matter before accepting fixed price?

**S:** A client requested fixed-price delivery despite significant uncertainty.

**T:** I needed to identify what could realistically be committed.

**A:** I assessed scope clarity, requirements maturity, data quality, integration complexity, regulatory variation, dependencies, environments, migration, testing, acceptance criteria, and change-control mechanisms.

**R:** Commercial commitments could be aligned with actual delivery uncertainty.

**SME Probe:** What should fixed-price contracts avoid?

**Reflection:** Unbounded uncertainty hidden inside fixed commitments creates predictable conflict.

## 14. You must recommend build versus buy

**Question:** How would you evaluate build versus buy for Finance capability?

**S:** A business capability could be delivered through custom development or an external product.

**T:** I needed to make the choice based on enterprise value.

**A:** I evaluated strategic differentiation, standard capability, time-to-value, total cost, integration, data ownership, security, scalability, maintainability, vendor dependency, skills, and exit options.

**R:** The decision became transparent and architecture-led.

**SME Probe:** When can building be justified?

**Reflection:** Build should be intentional where differentiation or unmet requirements justify ownership.

## 15. Client asks for proof of value

**Question:** How would you prove the value of a Finance transformation before full rollout?

**S:** Leadership wanted confidence before committing to enterprise scale.

**T:** I needed to design a measurable proof.

**A:** I selected a representative process or business unit, defined baseline metrics, target outcomes, success criteria, adoption measures, control requirements, and a time-bound experiment.

**R:** The organization could evaluate actual evidence before scaling.

**SME Probe:** What makes a good proof of value?

**Reflection:** A proof should test the assumptions that matter most to the investment decision.

# Mastery Framework — LEAD

Use this 7-step model for presales, leadership, and decision-making scenarios:

### 1. LOCATE
Understand the opportunity, client context, business problem, stakeholders, and strategic drivers.

### 2. EXPLORE
Discover requirements, constraints, pain points, risks, evidence gaps, and decision criteria.

### 3. ARCHITECT
Shape capabilities, solution options, architecture principles, dependencies, and transition paths.

### 4. DIFFERENTIATE
Connect the solution to measurable value, industry context, evidence, and credible differentiation.

### 5. DECIDE
Evaluate trade-offs, assumptions, risk, investment, and reversibility to support a transparent decision.

### 6. COMMIT
Align commercial commitments, scope, ownership, governance, success measures, and delivery assumptions.

### 7. LEAD
Carry the architecture intent into execution, measure outcomes, learn, and improve the transformation.

**Memory line:**

> **Locate → Explore → Architect → Differentiate → Decide → Commit → Lead**

# Common Anti-Patterns

- Selling before understanding the problem.
- Promising unvalidated capabilities.
- Leading with product features.
- Competing only on price.
- Hiding delivery assumptions.
- Overpromising to win an opportunity.
- Treating RFP responses as feature catalogs.
- Ignoring commercial implications of architecture decisions.
- Providing recommendations without decision criteria.
- Creating unrealistic fixed-price commitments.
- Using generic demos.
- Attacking competitors instead of comparing evidence.
- Failing to connect presales commitments to delivery.
- Treating architecture leadership as document production.
- Confusing decisiveness with certainty.
